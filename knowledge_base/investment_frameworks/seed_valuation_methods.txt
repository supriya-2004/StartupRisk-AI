Valuing early-stage startups at seed and pre-seed is fundamentally different from valuing established companies because there are no meaningful financial metrics to discount. Several structured frameworks have been developed to bring rigor to what is otherwise highly subjective. The following describes the most widely used methods with worked examples calibrated to the India startup context.

The Berkus Method was developed by angel investor Dave Berkus and provides a structured way to assign valuation to pre-revenue startups based on five qualitative factors. Each factor can add up to a maximum value to the company's pre-money valuation. The original Berkus framework assigns a maximum of 500,000 USD per factor, though practitioners adjust this based on market and stage norms.

The five Berkus factors are as follows. First, sound idea, meaning a credible and defensible concept with a real customer problem, adds up to 500,000 USD. Second, prototype, meaning a working proof of concept that demonstrates technical feasibility, adds up to 500,000 USD. Third, quality management team, meaning experienced and complete founding team with relevant domain expertise, adds up to 500,000 USD. Fourth, strategic relationships, meaning signed partnerships, distribution agreements, or channel relationships with credible organizations, adds up to 500,000 USD. Fifth, product rollout or sales, meaning early revenue, paying beta customers, or committed purchase orders, adds up to 500,000 USD. Under this method, a startup that scores well on all five dimensions could justify a pre-money valuation of 2.5 million USD, while one with only an idea and a team might justify only 500,000 to 1 million USD.

For India context, applying Berkus at 0.5 to 1 crore rupees per category is a reasonable localization, placing pre-seed Indian startups in the 1 to 5 crore rupee pre-money valuation range for angel rounds.

The Scorecard Method, developed by Bill Payne, compares a startup to a reference average valuation for pre-revenue companies in the same region and sector. The regional and sector reference valuation is multiplied by adjustment factors across six dimensions. The dimensions and their weighting are as follows. Strength of the management team accounts for 30 percent of the adjustment. Size of the opportunity accounts for 25 percent. Strength of the product or technology accounts for 15 percent. Competitive environment accounts for 10 percent. Marketing, sales channels, and partnerships accounts for 10 percent. Need for additional investment accounts for 5 percent. Other factors including regulatory exposure or IP protection account for 5 percent.

In practice, an assessor rates each dimension on a scale from 0 to 2, where 1 is average. The weighted product of all ratings is then multiplied by the reference average valuation. For example, if the average pre-seed SaaS valuation in India is 4 crore rupees pre-money, and a startup scores 1.3 on team strength, 1.2 on opportunity size, 1.0 on product, 0.9 on competition, 1.1 on marketing, and average on the remaining factors, the weighted adjustment factor would be approximately 1.17, placing the pre-money valuation at approximately 4.7 crore rupees.

The Venture Capital Method works backwards from a projected exit valuation to determine what pre-money valuation is appropriate given the investor's return expectations. The steps are as follows. First, estimate the company's revenue or earnings at exit, typically in 5 to 7 years. Second, apply an appropriate revenue or earnings multiple for a company in that sector at that scale to derive an expected exit valuation. Third, discount the exit valuation back to present value using the VC's required rate of return, typically 30 to 50 percent annually for early-stage investments. Fourth, calculate how much ownership the investor's capital will represent at the post-money valuation needed to generate the required return.

Worked example using VC Method for a B2B SaaS startup raising 50 lakh rupees. Projected ARR at year 5: 15 crore rupees. Applied multiple for B2B SaaS at that scale: 6 times ARR, giving an exit valuation of 90 crore rupees. Required return for angel investor: 40 percent annually over 5 years, representing a required multiple of 5.4 times. Present value of the exit is 90 divided by 5.4, or approximately 16.7 crore rupees. For the investor's 50 lakh rupees to generate the required return, they need approximately 3 percent of the exit value, which means the post-money valuation should be approximately 50 lakh divided by 3 percent, or approximately 16.7 crore rupees. Post-money minus investment equals pre-money of approximately 16.2 crore rupees.

Market calibration is a fourth practical approach used by most practicing angel investors who lack the time for full quantitative analysis. It involves checking what comparable companies at similar stages in similar sectors have raised at and using those data points to triangulate a reasonable range. AngelList, Tracxn, and Venture Intelligence in India publish deal data that enables this comparison. Most Indian seed rounds for strong founding teams with early traction fall in the range of 3 to 15 crore rupees pre-money valuation.

The most important principle in early-stage valuation is that the valuation set at seed will become the floor for the Series A, so both founders and investors should avoid extreme overvaluation at seed that creates an impossible Series A target. A high-quality team with early traction is worth paying a fair price for; an overvalued seed investment sets the company up for a flat or down round, which damages morale and future fundraising significantly.
