Due diligence for early-stage startup investment involves structured inquiry across six primary domains: team, product, market, financials, legal and corporate structure, and intellectual property. The depth of due diligence appropriate at seed stage is less than at Series A or B, but the domains are the same. The following framework reflects the approach used by institutional seed-stage investors and formalized angel groups.

Team due diligence is the highest priority at early stage because the team is often the only durable asset in a pre-revenue company. Verify the academic and professional credentials listed by founders. Conduct structured reference checks with former employers, colleagues, professors, and if possible, previous investors who can speak to the founder's decision-making under pressure. Assess co-founder relationship stability by asking how the team met, how they make decisions when they disagree, and how equity was divided and why. Research online presence including LinkedIn profiles, GitHub contributions for technical founders, published writing or presentations, and any media coverage. For founding teams in regulated industries, verify that no founder has prior regulatory actions, defaults on major financial obligations, or unresolved legal disputes that could create company liability.

Product due diligence requires direct interaction with the product, not just a founder-led demonstration. Use the product yourself for tasks it was designed to perform. Note where friction exists and how it compares to incumbent alternatives. If the product has a technical component that requires specialized evaluation, engage a technical advisor to assess architectural soundness, scalability, and security. Review product roadmap and assess whether the prioritization reflects genuine understanding of customer needs versus founder preference. For hardware or manufacturing products, verify that working prototypes exist and that the engineering team has the expertise to take the design to production.

Market due diligence involves independent validation of the market size claims made in the pitch. Source primary market data from government statistics, industry associations, or your own conversations with potential customers. Speak with 5 to 10 potential customers independently of the founders to validate that the pain point described is real and that the frequency and intensity of the problem are as stated. Map the full competitive landscape using industry databases, Google searches, AngelList, and Tracxn to identify competitors the founders may have omitted. Assess the regulatory environment independently by consulting a lawyer with relevant sector expertise if the sector is regulated.

Financial due diligence at seed stage is limited because there is usually no meaningful financial history to audit. The key items to review are the current cap table to confirm ownership percentages and check for any unusual provisions, founder compensation relative to the amount being raised, any existing debt or convertible instruments already outstanding that will affect dilution, the financial model assumptions, particularly revenue growth rate and customer acquisition cost, and the use of funds plan to assess whether the round size is appropriate for the proposed milestones.

Legal and corporate due diligence covers incorporation documents, articles of association, and any shareholders agreements. Confirm that the company is incorporated in an appropriate jurisdiction for receiving investment. In India, most venture-backed startups incorporate as private limited companies under the Companies Act 2013. Check that all founders have signed employment agreements with IP assignment clauses. Review any existing contracts with customers, suppliers, or partners for unusual exclusivity, change-of-control provisions, or revenue share obligations. Confirm that ESOP pools, if established, are properly documented and that the number of options granted does not exceed the authorized pool size.

Intellectual property due diligence is particularly important for technology startups claiming proprietary technology as a competitive advantage. Confirm ownership of all code in the company's codebase, including any components developed by contractors, by verifying IP assignment agreements were signed at the time of engagement. Check that no code in the product was copied or derived from open-source software without appropriate license compliance. If the company has filed or plans to file patents, have a patent attorney review the filings for claim quality and freedom to operate relative to existing patents in the domain. For startups using pre-trained AI models, confirm that commercial use is permitted under the model's license.

Customer reference checks are underutilized but highly valuable. With the founder's consent, speak with two to three existing customers or pilot users to confirm their account of why they chose the product, what alternatives they considered, how satisfied they are, and whether they would expand their usage or refer others. Discrepancies between the founder's characterization and customer accounts are a significant diligence flag. Very positive customer references independently confirming the founder's claims significantly increase investment confidence.

The due diligence process should conclude with a clear investment memo summarizing the findings across each domain, the identified key risks with an assessment of their mitigation, the terms being considered and their rationale, and the investor's conviction on the opportunity. Sharing a version of this memo with co-investors facilitates the syndication process and creates documentation for future reference if the investment is subsequently reviewed.
