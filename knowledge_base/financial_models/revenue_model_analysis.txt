Revenue model selection is one of the most consequential early decisions a startup makes because it determines unit economics, customer relationships, capital requirements, and ultimately the ceiling of the business. The following describes the eight most common revenue models in the technology startup context, with their structural advantages, limitations, and relevant benchmarks.

1. Subscription software, also known as SaaS. Customers pay a recurring monthly or annual fee for continuous access to software. This model generates predictable, compounding revenue and is the dominant model for B2B software. Gross margins are typically 70 to 85 percent once scaled. Key success factors are low churn, high net revenue retention from upsells, and a CAC payback period below 18 months. The primary challenge is that the subscription model requires building trust before customers commit, making the first 6 to 12 months of customer acquisition difficult. Annual upfront contracts, which many B2B SaaS companies offer at a 10 to 20 percent discount, improve cash flow and reduce churn. Best suited for workflow software, business intelligence tools, collaboration platforms, and any solution where continuous access is necessary for business operations.

2. Marketplace transaction fee. The platform takes a percentage of each transaction between buyers and sellers. Revenue scales with GMV rather than customer count, creating potential for very large revenue at scale. Gross margins are typically 50 to 75 percent depending on the degree of transaction processing and support services embedded in the fee. The primary challenge is the cold start problem described in the marketplace section. Take rates typically range from 2 to 30 percent depending on category and competitive dynamics. Best suited for labor marketplaces, product marketplaces, real estate platforms, and financial transaction intermediaries.

3. Transactional or usage-based pricing. Customers pay per use, per API call, per message sent, or per unit processed rather than a flat subscription fee. This model has gained significant traction in infrastructure, communications, and AI services companies. Twilio, AWS, Stripe, and OpenAI all use variants of this model. The advantage is extremely low friction for adoption because customers start at zero cost, and revenue scales directly with customer success. The challenge is revenue unpredictability and customer ability to reduce usage during budget pressure. Gross margins are typically 55 to 70 percent due to variable compute or infrastructure costs. Net revenue retention in best-in-class usage-based companies exceeds 130 percent as successful customers naturally expand consumption over time.

4. Freemium. A base product is offered for free with limited features or usage, and a premium paid tier provides full functionality. Freemium is primarily a customer acquisition strategy rather than a revenue model, and it only functions well when the free tier provides genuine value that attracts a large top-of-funnel, while the paid tier provides features that a meaningful percentage of users need badly enough to pay for. Conversion rates from free to paid in well-designed freemium products range from 2 to 5 percent for consumer products and 10 to 25 percent for B2B products. The risk is that a poorly designed freemium tier is either too good, converting no one to paid, or not good enough, attracting no users. Dropbox and Slack built their businesses on freemium; Evernote's struggle to convert free users to paid is a cautionary example.

5. Advertising and sponsored content. Revenue comes from brands paying to reach the platform's user base. This model requires very large, engaged user bases to generate meaningful revenue, because CPMs even on premium platforms rarely exceed 200 to 500 rupees per thousand impressions in India. Gross margins are high, typically 60 to 80 percent, but the model is volatile, as demonstrated by Facebook and Twitter facing significant revenue pressure when advertising markets contract. This model is viable primarily for consumer platforms with millions of active users, and is generally inappropriate as a primary model for early-stage B2B or niche consumer startups.

6. Licensing and royalty model. Intellectual property including patents, software, content, or branded assets is licensed to third parties who pay upfront fees or ongoing royalties. This model is most common in media, entertainment, enterprise software, and pharmaceutical sectors. It requires significant IP development investment upfront but can generate highly passive recurring revenue at scale. The challenge for early-stage startups is that licensing revenue depends on the IP being broadly valuable enough that others will pay to use it, which takes time to establish.

7. Hardware with software services, also known as the razor-and-blades model. Physical hardware is sold at near-cost or subsidized to drive adoption of higher-margin software services, consumables, or data subscriptions. Peloton, Oura Ring, agricultural IoT sensors, and industrial equipment companies use this model. Gross margins on hardware components are typically 20 to 40 percent, while associated software services margins are 60 to 80 percent. The overall blended margin and LTV depend critically on the attach rate and retention of the software service layer. Hardware startups face significant additional risks including supply chain complexity, quality control, certification costs, and working capital requirements for inventory.

8. Data and analytics services. The platform collects data as a byproduct of its core service and monetizes it by selling aggregated or enriched data to third parties, or by providing analytics services to participants in its ecosystem. Credit bureaus, market research firms, and health data aggregators use this model. This revenue stream is often additive to a primary SaaS or transaction revenue model rather than a standalone model at startup stage. Regulatory complexity around data monetization, particularly under GDPR-equivalent frameworks, is a significant constraint that startups must evaluate carefully before building a business that depends on data resale.

Revenue model selection should be driven by customer behavior and willingness to pay, not by founder preference for a particular model's economics. The best revenue models are ones customers find natural and fair given the value they receive. Multi-model approaches, such as combining a subscription base with usage-based expansion pricing, are common at scale and can be the optimal design once initial product-market fit is established in a single model.
