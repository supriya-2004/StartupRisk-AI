Marketplace businesses connect two or more sides, typically buyers and sellers, and generate revenue by facilitating transactions between them. The unit economics of marketplace businesses differ fundamentally from SaaS models because marketplaces earn revenue as a percentage of gross merchandise value rather than from subscriptions, and their primary challenges are achieving liquidity and managing the cold start problem rather than managing churn.

Take rate, also called the commission rate or rake, is the percentage of each transaction that a marketplace retains as revenue. Take rates vary significantly by category and competitive dynamics. Financial transaction marketplaces like PayPal typically charge 2 to 3 percent. B2C product marketplaces like Amazon and Flipkart charge 5 to 15 percent depending on category. Gig economy marketplaces like Uber and Rapido charge 15 to 25 percent. Professional services marketplaces like Upwork charge 20 to 25 percent. Food delivery platforms like Zomato and Swiggy charge restaurants 20 to 30 percent. High-value, low-frequency categories like real estate command very high take rates of 2 to 6 percent of transaction value because even a small percentage represents significant absolute revenue. Digital goods and services marketplaces like app stores charge 15 to 30 percent.

The key drivers of take rate sustainability are the value the marketplace provides relative to the alternatives, the concentration of power on each side of the market, and the presence or absence of direct competitor marketplaces offering lower fees. Marketplaces that provide significant trust and safety infrastructure, guaranteed payment processing, discovery services, and dispute resolution can justify higher take rates. Marketplaces that face easy disintermediation, where buyers and sellers can easily connect directly after meeting on the platform, must invest heavily in making the platform transaction more convenient and trustworthy than off-platform alternatives.

Gross merchandise value is the total value of transactions facilitated by the marketplace before the take rate is applied. GMV is an important scale metric but can be misleading as a business performance indicator because take rates and gross margins vary widely. A marketplace with 100 crore rupees GMV at a 5 percent take rate generates 5 crore rupees in revenue, while a marketplace with 20 crore rupees GMV at a 20 percent take rate generates 4 crore rupees in revenue. Investors in marketplace businesses assess revenue growth and gross profit, not GMV alone, though GMV provides context on market penetration.

Liquidity is the marketplace equivalent of product-market fit. A marketplace has liquidity when buyers can reliably find what they are looking for and sellers can reliably find buyers, typically within a reasonable time frame. Measuring liquidity requires category and geography-specific tracking of fill rates, which is the percentage of buyer requests that result in a successful match, time-to-match, and cancellation or abandonment rates. A marketplace without liquidity is not a marketplace product; it is a directory. Building to liquidity requires achieving a critical mass of supply on one side of the market before opening demand, which is the fundamental reason why marketplace fundraising is typically structured to fund supply acquisition before demand activation.

The cold start problem is the primary challenge for new marketplace entrants. The cold start problem arises because buyers come to a marketplace seeking supply, and sellers join a marketplace seeking buyers, but neither side will join without the other already present. Solutions to the cold start problem that have been validated by successful marketplace companies include the following. First, starting in a single, densely bounded geography or category and achieving true liquidity there before expanding, which is how Uber launched city by city. Second, manually seeding one side of the marketplace, such as Airbnb's founders manually photographing early listings to improve supply quality and attract buyers. Third, solving for one side completely with a non-marketplace tool before introducing the other side, as OpenTable built restaurant reservation software for restaurants before launching the consumer booking platform. Fourth, subsidizing the scarcer side of the market to overcome the chicken-and-egg problem.

Unit economics benchmarks for marketplace businesses include contribution margin after variable costs of transaction processing, customer support, and fulfillment, which should be positive on a per-transaction basis for a healthy marketplace. Customer acquisition cost for both supply and demand sides must be tracked separately. Retention on both sides must be measured. For supply-side retention in a services marketplace, repeat job acceptance rate measures platform stickiness. For demand-side retention, repeat purchase rate and cohort revenue retention provide the key signals.

Take rate expansion over time is a marker of marketplace power and is a common growth lever for mature platforms. As a marketplace gains scale and becomes the dominant discovery channel for a category, it can gradually increase take rates because sellers become dependent on the platform for customer acquisition. Amazon's seller fees have increased multiple times, as have App Store commissions, demonstrating this dynamic. However, take rate increases carry disintermediation risk if alternatives become viable.

India-specific marketplace context includes the rapid growth of aggregator and hyperlocal marketplace models in sectors including food delivery, home services, beauty, automotive services, and logistics. ONDC, the Open Network for Digital Commerce backed by the government, represents a structural shift that allows any technology platform to access a shared commerce network, potentially reducing the competitive moat of dominant marketplace platforms. Startups building marketplace businesses in India should assess their ONDC strategy as both a threat and an opportunity.
