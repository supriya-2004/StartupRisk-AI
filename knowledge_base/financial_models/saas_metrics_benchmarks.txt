SaaS financial metrics have been extensively benchmarked by investors, analysts, and operators over the past two decades. The following benchmarks are drawn from public SaaS company data, OpenView Partners annual SaaS benchmarks, Bessemer Venture Partners state of cloud reports, and SaaS Capital research covering thousands of private SaaS companies.

Customer acquisition cost is the total sales and marketing expense in a period divided by the number of new customers acquired in that same period. For accurate CAC measurement, the time-lag between marketing spend and customer acquisition must be accounted for, as campaigns executed in one quarter often generate customers in the following quarter. Fully-loaded CAC should include all fully-loaded salaries of sales and marketing personnel, not just advertising spend. Benchmarks for CAC by customer segment are as follows. Self-service SMB SaaS products should have CAC below 500 USD per customer. Inside sales SMB products typically have CAC between 1,000 and 5,000 USD. Mid-market products sold through an inside sales team have CAC of 5,000 to 25,000 USD. Enterprise field sales products have CAC of 25,000 to over 100,000 USD.

Customer lifetime value is the total revenue expected from a customer over the entire duration of their relationship with the company. For subscription businesses, LTV is calculated as average revenue per account divided by gross margin multiplied by the churn rate. For example, a SaaS company with 10,000 USD average annual contract value, 75 percent gross margin, and 10 percent annual churn rate has an LTV of 10,000 multiplied by 0.75 divided by 0.10, or 75,000 USD. The LTV calculation is highly sensitive to churn rate, which means that small improvements in retention have a compounding positive effect on LTV.

The LTV to CAC ratio is the most widely used efficiency metric in SaaS. A ratio of 3 to 1 or higher is considered healthy, meaning the lifetime value of a customer is at least 3 times the cost to acquire them. Top-quartile SaaS companies target LTV to CAC ratios of 5 to 1 or higher. Ratios below 1 to 1 indicate a fundamentally broken unit economics model. Ratios between 1 and 3 indicate either a high-churn or high-CAC problem that must be addressed before scaling. At early stage, founders often have insufficient data to calculate LTV precisely, in which case the proxy metric of 12-month net revenue retention per cohort is a reasonable substitute.

Monthly recurring revenue growth benchmarks by stage inform whether a company is on track for venture investment. For pre-seed and seed companies, the minimum viable benchmark is 10 to 20 percent month-over-month growth in the first 6 to 12 months after launch. At Series A stage, with 1 to 3 million USD in ARR, benchmark growth is 200 to 300 percent year-over-year. At Series B, with 5 to 15 million USD in ARR, benchmark growth is 150 to 200 percent year-over-year. The T2D3 framework, coined by Tomasz Tunguz, describes the ideal SaaS growth trajectory as tripling ARR for two consecutive years followed by doubling for three consecutive years, taking a company from roughly 1 million to 100 million USD in ARR.

Annual churn rate benchmarks vary significantly by customer segment. Enterprise SaaS, with customers at annual contract values above 50,000 USD, should have annual logo churn below 5 percent. Mid-market SaaS should have annual logo churn below 10 percent. SMB SaaS faces structurally higher churn due to customer business failures, budget sensitivity, and lower switching costs, with benchmarks of 10 to 20 percent annual logo churn. Gross revenue churn measures the dollar value of lost revenue, and best-in-class SaaS companies keep gross revenue churn below 5 percent annually regardless of segment by focusing on successful customer outcomes.

Net revenue retention includes expansion revenue from existing customers through upselling and cross-selling, making it possible to achieve NRR above 100 percent even if some customers churn. World-class NRR benchmarks by segment are as follows. Infrastructure and platform SaaS companies like Twilio and Snowflake regularly achieve NRR of 130 to 150 percent. Horizontal software tools achieve NRR of 110 to 130 percent. Vertical software solutions achieve NRR of 100 to 115 percent. SMB-focused products typically achieve NRR of 90 to 110 percent.

The Rule of 40 is the primary efficiency benchmark for mature SaaS companies and is increasingly applied at growth stage. The rule states that a company's ARR growth rate percentage plus its free cash flow margin percentage should equal or exceed 40. For example, a company growing at 60 percent ARR year-over-year with a negative 20 percent free cash flow margin scores 40 and passes. A company growing at 25 percent with zero free cash flow margin scores only 25 and is considered underperforming relative to benchmark. Top-performing public SaaS companies like HubSpot and Datadog have consistently scored above 50 on the Rule of 40. For early-stage companies, growth rate should dominate the formula, with efficiency becoming more important as growth naturally moderates.

Sales efficiency metrics including the magic number and payback period provide additional diagnostic value. The magic number is calculated as quarterly revenue added multiplied by 4, divided by the prior quarter's sales and marketing expense. A magic number above 0.75 indicates healthy sales efficiency. Above 1.0 indicates the company should increase marketing spend aggressively. Below 0.5 indicates a sales or product problem that should be resolved before increasing spend. CAC payback period is the most intuitive efficiency metric: at what point does cumulative gross profit from a new customer equal the cost to acquire them. The benchmark is 18 months or below for high-efficiency SaaS businesses.
