Y Combinator has funded over 4,000 companies since 2005, including Airbnb, Stripe, Dropbox, DoorDash, Coinbase, Instacart, and Reddit. The combined valuation of YC-funded companies exceeds 600 billion USD. YC has developed one of the most extensively documented frameworks for evaluating early-stage startups, and its selection criteria reflect decades of pattern recognition about what distinguishes fundable early-stage companies from those that will struggle.

YC accepts approximately 1.5 to 2 percent of applicants. In a typical batch of 200 to 250 companies, applications number between 15,000 and 20,000. The acceptance rate has been declining as the applicant pool grows, making the signals YC looks for increasingly important to understand for any early-stage investor or founder.

The team is the primary evaluation criterion. YC partners have repeatedly stated that they fund people more than ideas because ideas change but teams persist. The ideal YC founding team is two to four people with complementary skills, usually at least one strong technical builder and one person with the commercial instinct to talk to customers and sell. Teams that have worked together before, or who have a personal relationship predating the company, are viewed more favorably. YC explicitly prefers founders who know their domain deeply, either through professional experience or through being the target customer themselves.

The idea matters, but not in the way most applicants assume. YC does not require a finished product or even a clear business model at application stage. What they look for is evidence that the founder has identified a real problem and has thought carefully about why now is the right time to solve it. The most compelling YC applications describe a specific narrow problem in vivid detail, usually because the founder experienced the problem personally. Broad mission statements without a concrete initial use case are a negative signal.

Traction, even early or informal, disproportionately influences YC decisions. Evidence that real people have asked for the product, are using early versions, or are willing to pay before the product is finished is highly weighted. This can include pre-signed letters of intent, pilot customers paying nominal amounts, or interview quotes from 10 to 20 potential customers describing their existing workaround and expressing willingness to switch. The key signal is that the founder has done the work to validate demand before building, rather than building and hoping demand emerges.

The ability to get things done quickly is a core trait YC looks for. In the YC application, one question asks about what the founding team has built or accomplished. Teams that have shipped meaningful products, completed unusual projects, or demonstrated the ability to execute ambitious goals outside of a formal organizational context are viewed more favorably than teams whose only credential is a prestigious university degree or employer without tangible output.

Growth potential is a necessary but not sufficient condition. YC funds companies that have the potential to be very large businesses, which is why ideas that are too locally constrained or that serve very small markets are typically rejected regardless of team quality. However, YC explicitly advises founders not to start with a large market and work backwards, but instead to find a small group of users who love the product intensely and then expand from that core. The famous YC test is whether there are any users who would be very disappointed if the product disappeared.

Common traits of the most successful YC companies include founders who have obsessive domain knowledge, products that are dramatically better at the primary use case rather than marginally better across many features, business models with natural virality or word-of-mouth distribution built into the product experience, and founding teams with the psychological resilience to persist through periods of slow growth without losing conviction. Airbnb's founders famously sold custom cereal boxes to stay afloat before their growth inflected; this kind of resourceful persistence is a characteristic YC deeply values.

YC advises its portfolio companies to focus on the following post-investment priorities. First, launch quickly and talk to users every single day. Second, measure one primary metric and make decisions based on its trajectory. Third, do things that do not scale in the early stage, including manual customer success, personal sales calls, and hand-holding early users, because these activities generate the learning needed to eventually build scalable systems. Fourth, keep team size small until product-market fit is demonstrated. Fifth, avoid premature optimization of systems, processes, and culture before the core product value proposition is proven.
